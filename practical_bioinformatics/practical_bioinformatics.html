Preface

The field of bioinformatics is advancing at a remarkable rate. With the rapid development of new analytical techniques that use the latest advances in machine learning and data science, today’s biologists are gaining fantastic new insights into the natural world’s most complex systems. An increasing number of students are interested in learning bioinformatics, but most of the bioinformatics textbooks are theory-based and lack a lab-based manual that can assist them in handling large biological data. Therefore, it is necessary and urgent to have a lab manual that can be integrated with bioinformatics courses. This book is aimed at students, faculty, and researchers in biology, health/medical sciences, agricultural sciences, bioengineering, biotechnology, and genetic engineer- ing. The content should be easily accessible to undergraduate students, faculty, and general readers interested in biology or bioinformatics. As a motivating example, the first ten chapters focus on bioinformatics approaches to the study of antibiotic resistance. No previous experience in bioinfor- matics is assumed. The last two chapters focus on the use of R to analyze and solve biological questions. Users need to install R or R studio and related packages on a local computer. Again, no previous experience in R/R studio is needed. All examples and programming codes in this book are designed for beginners. These 12 chapters can be used as the basis for a 6- to 12-week laboratory module. This book can be used either stand-alone, or in conjunction with a lecture course.

Chapter 1 Introduction to Bioinformatics

1.1 What Is Bioinformatics?

The three greatest projects of the twentieth century are the Manhattan project (1942-1945), the Apollo project (1961-1972), and human genome project (1990-2003). Each one had its own merits. The human genome project was an international scientific research project, which aimed to determine human DNA sequence and gene function. It was formally launched in 1990 and completed in April 2003. After the human genome was completed, many other organisms such as Arabidopsis, mouse, and zebra fish have been sequenced and analyzed. In July 2025, the US National Center for Biotechnology Information contained complete geno- mic sequences of 53,232 eukaryotes (animals, fungi, and plants), 2,692,439 pro- karyotes (bacteria), 230,411 viruses, a well as many sequences of organellar genome (chloroplast and mitochondiron), plasmids, and individual genes. Until the 1980s, bioinformatics was not a widely used term. The most common terms were biocomputing or computational biology. Bioinformatics appears to have been coined by Paulien Hogeweg and Ben Hesper in the early 1970s,1 who defined it as “the study of informatic processes in biotic systems.” Many of the earliest uses, however, were exclusively in Dutch, so the term did not become common in English until later. My personal recollection is of hearing it at the “Macromolecules, Genes and Computers” workshop in 1986. There was considerable resistance to the label because many people felt bioinformatics was restricted to topics related to informa- tion storage and retrieval, although this was clearly not the intent of Hogeweg and Hesper. In any case, in spite of the difficulty of referring to oneself as a bioinformaticist, which doesn’t easily roll off the tongue, it did become the dominant name for the field. Along the way, the definition reverted to something much closer to the original. Currently, almost any computational analysis of macromolecules

1.2 Sequence Technology

Bioinformatics has been driven by the development of sequencing technology, which allows scientists to decipher the secrets of DNA sequences. The first- generation of sequence technology was invented by two different groups. One was a selective cleavage method developed by Allan Maxam and Walter Gilbert (usually referred to as Maxam-Gilbert sequencing), the other was a sequencing by synthesis method developed by Frederick Sanger and coworkers (referred to as chain termi- nation, dideoxy, or Sanger sequencing). Maxam-Gilbert sequencing is no longer in use, while modifications of Sanger’s sequencing by the synthesis approach remain widely used. First-generation sequencing was a manual process that involved hand- loading samples onto large polyacrylamide gels. First generation methods were eventually automated with separation of DNA fragments using gel or capillary electrophoresis. Sequencers of this generation, such as the Applied Biosystems PRISM, was the workhorses in producing the human genome (2001). Second-generation sequencing marked the first massively parallel approach, and was the first approach that did not require amplifying and purifying individual sequence fragments. In pyrosequencing (also known as 454 sequencing), DNA was randomly attached to beads using “adapter” sequences and amplified in situ. The beads were passed over a flowcell with wells that could hold just one bead. The sequencing by synthesis process then proceeded by passing one nucleotide at a time through the flowcell and measuring the amount of pyrophosphate released. How- ever, automated dideoxy sequencing (ABI) and 454 pyrosequencing, were soon replaced by next-generation sequencing (NGS). Currently, next generation sequencing, NGS, mostly refers to Illumina sequenc- ing. NGS is massively parallel, sequencing millions of fragments (usually the length is 100-300 bp) simultaneously per run. This process translates into sequencing hundreds to thousands of genes at one time. With NGS methods, whole genomes can be rapidly sequenced. For instance, large plant and animal genomes can be completed in days. NGS methods can also be used to sequence messenger RNA (technically a cDNA copy of a RNA). The technique, known as RNA-Seq, can discover novel RNA variants and splice sites or quantify mRNAs for gene expres- sion analysis. NGS is the main cause of the explosion in the number of organisms whose genomes and transcriptomes have been sequenced and analyzed. This tech- nology is currently the most successful and widely used, but long-read sequencing is seeing increasing usage.

Chapter 2 Introduction to Antibiotic Resistance

Antibiotic resistance, also known as antimicrobial resistance (AMR), is one of the most serious medical problems facing the world today. After a century in which death from bacterial infection was relatively rare, we now face the possibility of returning to the time of our great-grandparents, in which 50% or more of deaths could be due to bacterial infection. Bioinformatics plays a huge role in studying antibiotic resistance. It can be used to identify antibiotic resistance genes, the origin of resistance genes, the mechanism of antibiotic resistance, and, sometimes, possible alternative treatments. This chapter introduces the concept and mechanisms of antibiotic resistance, which we use as an example of how bioinformatics analyses can be used to solve biological questions.

2.3 Causes of Resistance

The use of antibiotics is the cause of antibiotic resistance. In biological terms, antibiotic treatment is a strong selective pressure. Most bacteria are killed by treatment with antibiotics, but occasionally, a mutation occurs that makes a bacte- rium resistant. The resistant strain can then spread rapidly since the antibiotic treatment has killed off all competing bacteria. Since the discovery of penicillin in 1928, a steady stream of antibiotic compounds has been discovered or synthesized and brought to market (Table 2.2). However, in recent years, the discovery of new antibiotics has slowed dramatically; only one completely new class of antibiotic has been introduced since 1968, and most “new” antibiotics are modifications or com- binations of already known classes. While any use of antibiotics tends to produce resistance over time, the situation is exacerbated by the widespread overuse of antibiotics. Even though antibiotics are

2.4 Mechanisms of Resistance

Antibiotics have many different mechanisms, but most commonly act by inhibiting enzymes important for cell wall biosynthesis, protein synthesis, transcription, DNA replication, or synthesis of critical metabolites (Table 2.3). Interference with the permeability or integrity of the bacterial cell membrane is another mechanism. The mechanisms of resistance to antibiotics are as diverse as the mechanisms of the antibiotics themselves, but can be grouped into general categories: decreased uptake/transport of the antibiotic, efflux of the antibiotic or a derivative, breakdown or modification of the antibiotic or a derivative, or mutation/modification of the target molecule. In general, resistance requires only one or a small number of genes. When resistance genes are transferred to episomes (plasmids), small independently replicating circular DNAs found in bacteria, they are very easily transferred between bacteria-even between distantly related species. Drug resistance genes on episomes have been directly linked to many types of antibiotic resistance. Plasmids, small, independently replicating circles of DNA in bacteria, are directly linked to antibiotic resistance. Typically, once the gene or genes necessary for resistance are transferred to a plasmid, the plasmid spreads rapidly through the population. Plasmids may be transferred between bacteria by conjugation, by trans- duction (transfer by a bacteriophage), or by release to the environment and direct uptake. Chapter 3 Introduction to Evolution and Homology

Evolution and the relationships between macromolecules, as seen in their nucleotide and protein sequences, are the foundation of sequence analysis. Understanding how the evolutionary process works is key to understanding how we compare sequences and how we identify sequences with an evolutionary relationship. The inheritance of genes and their encoded proteins from common ancestors, with only limited changes due to evolution, means that such genes/proteins (homologs) have similar structure and function. Determining whether gene/protein sequences are homologous is therefore a critical issue in bioinformatics. In turn, determining whether sequences are homologs depends on our confidence that the degree of observed sequence similarity would not be observed by chance. This chapter will discuss the concepts of evolution, homology, and how to determine whether genes/proteins are homologous.

Evolution requires two simple processes to act together: One process generates inheritable changes in the characteristics of organisms (mutation), and the other removes individuals with low fitness/poor adaptation to the environment (selection). Selection, the other essential process, removes individuals in the population based on their individual characteristics. This is usually discussed in terms of an individual’s fitness. Individ- uals with high fitness are more likely to survive and reproduce, thus passing their (possibly altered) characteristics on to the next generation. Without a mechanism to generate diversity, all individuals in the population are the same and are therefore equally fit. Hence, there is no evolutionary change. In the presence of diversity, selection acts to probabilistically remove less fit individuals, thereby increasing the frequency of characteristics that enhance fitness in the population. This focusing on the characteristics of the population around survival-important traits is convergent evolution.

3.3 Homologs, Mutations and the Molecular Clock

Most life on Earth uses DNA as its genetic material. Diversity is generated by mutations in the DNA sequence. Mutations can be generated by many processes, including chemical or radiation damage, incorrect repair of such damage, errors in DNA replication, the action of viruses or transposons that integrate into the DNA, or errors in the process of recombination. Mutation results in four kinds of changes: base substitutions, insertion or deletion of one or more bases, inversions of chromo- somal segments, and translocations of DNA segments between chromosomes. Although the mutation process has preferences for certain bases/DNA regions, mutation is relatively random with respect to the information content (the genes and their encoded proteins) of the DNA, and relatively proportional to time. This allows the number of mutational differences between the same gene in different species to be used as a molecular clock-the more mutations, the longer the time since the genes diverged from each other. The mutational molecular clock is an example of a stochastic clock. Another example of a stochastic clock is the Interna- tional Atomic Time (IAT) clock, which is accurate to about one part in 1016 (around a billionth of a second per year). Although stochastic clocks can be very accurate, high accuracy depends on observing a very large number of random events. The IAT clock, for instance, observes a specific electronic transition in Cs-133, which occurs 9,192,631,770/ sec. In the case of mutations, there are only a relatively small number of observable events-usually not even thousands. In addition, we cannot observe all the muta- tional changes-when multiple mutations affect the same base, all we can see when examining sequences from two species is that there has been at least one mutational change. We also do not see mutational changes that are not fixed in the popula- tion because the organism with the mutation did not survive and reproduce. These issues make the time estimates derived from molecular clocks very inconsistent

As evolution proceeds, a single parent species separates into multiple daughter species; the number of mutational differences between corresponding genes in the daughter species can be used to estimate the timing and mechanism of the speciation. Such corresponding genes, which derive from a common ancestor, are called homologs. Speciation is one way that genes can be separated and begin to diverge from each other. To distinguish these relationships, we use the terms orthologs, genes separated by a speciation event, and paralogs, genes separated by a gene duplication event. Homology, similarity in form and function between features in different species resulting from inheritance of a feature from a common ancestor, is a critical concept in bioinformatics. A feature might be, for instance, a protein (in this case, we say the proteins are homologs, or that they are homologous). Because of their descent from a common ancestor, homologs are likely to have similar structure and function.

A more technical way to express this is to say we need to know the expected distribution of comparison scores for unrelated sequences-when looking at potential homologs, if we observe comparison scores that are much higher than expected for unrelated sequences, we can conclude the genes/proteins we are looking at are related. When studying an evolutionary process, such as the spread of antibiotic resis- tance, it is critical that we look at sequences that are homologous-if they are not homologous, the estimates and evolutionary models that we create will be meaning- less. The simplest kind of query is a keyword query, which searches for a word or phrase in the body of stored information. Keyword queries can be powerful, but if you lack experience in a field, you may find it difficult to identify the correct keywords. For instance, searching the NCBI PubMed literature database for “muscle tumor” produces more than 75,000 results, but inspection of the results shows that many of the hits merely mention “muscle” and “tumor” in the same article. Searching for a more technical term, such as “fibrosarcoma,” produces almost 20,000 results, which are more closely related to muscle tumors but also include some skin tumors (dermatofibrosarcoma). Finding good keywords usually requires an iterative process of searching, examining the results, and refining your query. Most database management systems can combine query search terms to produce more powerful queries using the Boolean AND, OR, and NOT operators. Queries with multiple words can be interpreted in different ways; clearly the search for “muscle tumor,” above, is interpreted as muscle OR tumor, that is, show me all articles containing the words muscle or the word tumor. Boolean expressions are interpreted left to right (similar to addition), but the order of the operations can be controlled by parentheses (just as in algebra). Here’s an example. Chapter 5 Investigating Sequence Comparison Statistics

From previous chapters, students have already obtained the sequences of the genes and proteins related to their aoi. In order to obtain more information about these genes/proteins, such as their evolutionary relationships, it is useful to obtain the gene/ protein sequences of related genes/proteins. This is important because the conserved regions of homologs are closely related to the structure and function of the mole- cules. To determine if genes are homologous, we need to determine if their sequences are more similar than we would expect them to be by chance. When we compare two sequences, we measure how similar/different the sequences are. Our conclusion about whether the genes/proteins are homologous are drawn based on hypothesis testing. We propose the null hypothesis that these sequences are random vs the alternative hypothesis that these sequences are related. Therefore, it is important to understand the statistical background of sequence comparison. Normal distributions are often used in statistical analyses, however, for sequence comparison, it follows an extreme value distribution (EVD), which we will learn in this lab.

Step 1: Index the database so that short fixed-length words can be rapidly looked up. This index is saved, so this step only needs to be done once. 8.2 Background

Multiple sequence alignment (MSA) has been a very active topic of bioinformatics almost from the beginning. Exactly solving the MSA problem is extremely difficult, so all major methods rely on an approximation called progressive alignment. In this approach, we first determine the distance between each pair of sequences. We use these distances to construct a tree in which the most similar sequences lie close

Beginning with the most similar sequences, we align them to each other. Because these sequences are very similar, they have the lowest chance of having incorrect regions in their alignments. After aligning these sequences, their alignment is frozen, and we then align the MSA containing the two sequences to the next closest sequence. By frozen, we mean that the alignment between the first two sequences is not allowed to change, although insertions and deletions with respect to the new sequence can be added. This process is continued, always aligning the closest sequences first, until all sequences have been added to the MSA. The progressive alignment approach is fast and can be used to align thousands of sequences, if necessary. The drawback to the basic progressive alignment strategy has to do with the freezing of the current MSA before adding each new sequence. If the alignments could be guaranteed to be correct at each step, this would not be a problem. Unfortunately, this is not the case, and errors that occur in the early steps are then “frozen in” and carried through all the way to the final alignment. In the last 15 years, a new generation of MSA programs has addressed this issue by adding an additional step in which subsets of sequences are removed from the MSA, unfrozen, and realigned to the MSA. In many cases, while a pairwise alignment might contain some errors, the alignment of one or a few sequences to the entire group is more likely to be correct. The MSA program that we will use in this lab, Clustal Omega, uses this enhanced progressive alignment strategy.

Because motifs are directly and causally related to protein structure and function, we are interested in identifying motifs, especially in novel sequences with unknown function. There are three basic approaches, each one more complex but also more powerful than the last: consensus sequences, regular expressions, and position-specific scoring matrices (PSSM). The oldest and weakest method is the consensus sequence method. Consensus sequences were first introduced to define DNA sequence motifs such as promoters and operators. The definition is very simple-a consensus sequence is a string of letters that represents the majority rule character at each column of an MSA. Matching to consensus sequences is also simple-just slide the motif across a sequence and note the positions where the letters of in the consensus sequence match all the letters in the sequence of interest. The problem is that consensus sequences lose important information present in the MSA; in particular, they do not distinguish positions that are completely conserved (inference: positions that are critical for function) and positions where one of the letters has only a slight plurality (inference: not very important for function). The big advantage of consensus sequences is that they are simple to display and remember. Senior molecular biologists often know dozens of consensus sequences. Because of the simplicity and clarity of displaying consensus sequences, they are often shown even when matching sequences are identified by more powerful methods. Regular expressions (RE) are an improvement over consensus sequences and can represent all consensus sequences (they are a superset of consensus sequences). RE are still a string representation, but they allow more than one letter to match at each position. This retains much more of the information present in the MSA and distinguishes those columns where multiple letters are observed in the MSA. RE also allow positions to be unconserved, that is, to match to any letter in a sequence (typically represented as N in nucleic acid sequences or X in protein sequences). However, some information is still lost; although two letters are present at a particular column in an MSA, one may be much more dominant than the other (indicating its greater functional importance). RE also allow a limited amount of variation in the spacing between conserved letters (which would be seen as inser- tions in the MSA). The big advantage of RE is that they have been the subject of intense study in computer science for decades, and very efficient methods are known for identifying them in large texts (such as genome databases). One well-known protein sequence motif is the zinc finger motif. It is a protein structural motif that allows proteins to interact with DNA, RNA, or other proteins. One of the important uses of multiple sequence alignments is in constructing phylogenetic trees (see Chap. 10). They also provide a huge amount of information about which regions of a protein family are conserved, and therefore, which regions are most important for its structure and function. There are a number of ways that protein structure and function give rise to evolutionary constraints. The most general effect has to do with the partitioning of the hydrophilic (water-loving) and hydrophobic (water-fearing) residues between the interior and exterior of the protein structure. The interior of folded proteins is densely packed, comparable to an organic solid, and denser than an organic liquid such as octanol. It is energetically unfavorable to bury polarizable groups in the tightly packed internal core of the protein; that is, a mutation that would bury a polarizable hydrophilic group destabilizes the structure and interferes with protein folding. As an analogy, you can imagine trying to force a polarizable molecule, water, into a hydrophobic solvent such as vegetable oil. We all know that this is difficult, and left to itself, the water and oil will separate. It is even more disruptive to bury charged residues (lysine, arginine, glutamate, and aspartate) in the interior of a protein, except in the special case where a positively charged residue interacts with a negative one to form a salt bridge.2

For those who would like a little more detail: The reason for the partitioning of hydrophilic and hydrophobic residues largely has to do with entropy, which is a measure of disorder. Hydrophobic molecules in water tend to be surrounded by an organized “cage” of water molecules held together by hydrogen bonds. When a hydrophobic amino acid side chain moves from an aqueous (water) environment to the core of the protein, this cage of water is released, thereby increasing entropy. This positive entropy change has been called the hydrophobic effect and is one of the main driving forces of protein folding.

A second constraint arises due to the tight packing of the protein interior-a mutation that substitutes a large residue for a small residue, or vice versa, in the core of the protein tends to destabilize the protein structure. It’s easy to see why trying to force a large residue, such as phenylalanine, into the space occupied by a small residue, such as alanine, is difficult. The opposite case, a mutation that replaces a large residue with a small one, is equally disruptive because, without alterations to the folded structure, this would leave a cavity, a vacuum, in the middle of the protein. As we know, this is an unnatural and physically unlikely situation (horror vacui). What we see in a multiple alignment tends to follow these principles; the residues that are on the protein surface tend to be hydrophilic and accumulate more muta- tional changes. The residues that are buried in the core of the protein tend to be hydrophobic and to accumulate mutations more slowly. Phenetic and Cladistic Trees One of the long-term goals of biology has been to produce the tree of life-a diagram that shows how every organism is related to each other. skull to the width. Such trees, in which organisms are classified based on overall similarity, are called phenetic trees. At the end of the twentieth century, there was a huge debate between advocates of phenetics and advocates of cladistics. A cladistic classification attempts to group organisms according to their ancestral relationships, with more closely related organisms in narrower systematic groups. One of the primary principles of a cladistics classification is monophyly, the idea that all members of a taxonomic grouping should be closer to each other than they are to any outside groups. Such a group, which comprises all organisms descended from a common ancestor, is also called a clade. This seems like an obvious and familiar principle; all primates are more closely related to each other than any are to canids or equids. Clearly, the traditional Linnaean classification system, with its division into kingdoms, phyla, classes, orders, etc., contains many polyphyletic groups and is not cladistic. For instance, we know that mammals are descended from a reptile, and reptiles from fish, but the traditional classification makes fish, reptiles, and mammals all classes of equal rank.

The problem with using morphology (overall physical similarity) as a basis for classification is that it is difficult to weigh the importance of one feature vs another. Which is more important, for instance, the presence of five toes or the presence of fur? Thus, when classifications are based on morphological characteristics, it is not that easy to distinguish the phenetic and cladistic perspectives. The debate between phenetics and cladistics, came to a head when the advent of sequencing methods made it possible to correctly determine ancestral relationships based on DNA and protein sequences. One of the reasons sequences are so much more powerful is that, to a first approximation, all sequence characters are on the same scale-there is much less need to weight the relative importance of characters (i.e., nucleotide bases and amino acid residues). Another advantage of sequence informa- tion is that hundreds or even thousands of characters (the DNA bases or amino acid residues) are available.

Constructing Distance Trees Conceptually, constructing a tree based on a matrix of distances is simple. Initially, every sequence (taxon) is considered to be in its own group. The general approach alternates between finding the two taxa with the shortest distance (greatest similarity) and joining them into a single group. Followed by recalculating the distances between the new group and the other taxa. These steps are then repeated until all the taxa have been joined into a single group (sometimes called an Operational Taxonomic Unit or OTU). A more detailed description of the construction of distance-based trees requires several steps:

Step 1: Full-length sequences spanning the phylogenetic range of interest must be selected. This will be done using Blast searches. Step 2: An MSA will be constructed based on the protein sequences. This alignment allows us to calculate the distance between each pair of sequences. The distance can be simply the number of positions at which the aligned sequences differ.

There are many other possible ways to calculate/update distances: One of the most useful called is Neighbor-Joining (NJ). Without going into the details of the calculation, it suffices to say that UPGMA does not take full advantage of the information in the distance matrix - for instance, in calculating the distance of the branch from the H/C ancestor to H and C, the information contained in the distances between G and H and G and C is not used. NJ uses this additional information and therefore makes better trees.

In an RNA-seq experiment, we assume most genes do not change; in other words, the null hypothesis is that the mean difference of gene expression between the control group and the treatment is zero. Clearly this condition cannot be me in many conditions, for instance when cells are infected by a pathogen and are dying, so more complicated analyses than the ones described here must be used. In a single RNA-seq experiment, we apply a statistical test for differential expression to thousands of genes simultaneously. This creates a multiple testing problem. The threshold α in a statistical test measures the probability of seeing a positive result (differential expression) when there are only random differences in the treatment and control conditions. α is thus a P-value, which is defined as the prob- ability of seeing a difference greater than a threshold when no significant difference is present (i.e., the datasets being compared have only random differences). If only random variation is present, all of the genes we predict to be differentially expressed are false positives. Consider an organism with 20,000 transcripts, if we choose α = 0.05, we expect to predict that 1000 genes are differentially expressed, even if the true number is zero. What if the true number of differentially expressed genes is 100? In that case, the fraction of genes, out of the ones we predict as positives, that are correct is 100 out of 1100 or 91%. The fraction of genes incorrectly predicted is 91%. This fraction is called the false discovery rate (FDR). In most cases, it’s clearly unacceptable for 91% of our results to be false positives. To reduce the FDR, we adjust the calculated P-values for multiple testing, so that our results achieve a specific FDR. FDR thresholds between 0.25 and 0.05 are common. Increasing the FDR always increases the number of false positives as well as the number of true positives detected (called “recall” in the statistical literature). Lowering the FDR decreases the number of false positives, but more true positives are also missed. There is no a priori best FDR threshold; an appropriate threshold depends on the cost of missing a true positive vs the cost of having a false positive. There are several methods that can control the false discovery rate (FDR), such as the Benjamini- Hochberg method and the Bonferroni method. Generally, the application of FDR control methods to data will be reported as FDR, adjusted P-value, or Q-value. In differential expression experiments, we measure two distinct aspects of the difference between the sample groups we are comparing (for instance a treatment group and an untreated control group). The first is a statistical effect, represented by the adjusted P-value, which estimates the probability that we can distinguish the expression level of a gene in our two groups. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) Large complex feature vectors, such as those seen in gene expression analysis, frequently contain many highly correlated elements. In a gene expression experi- ment these would be sets of genes that respond similarly to the treatment. In a typical differential gene expression analysis, many genes may have relative expression

levels that are similar across all experimental conditions. This distorts the pattern seen in clustering because the expression values are not independent, and the clustering result tends to be dominated by the largest groups of correlated values.

PCA tries to solve this problem by transforming the data to a new coordinate system in which the elements of the feature vector are uncorrelated (sometimes this is called independent or orthogonal, the meaning is the same). The new coordinate system (referred to as the principal components) is a weighted linear combination of the elements of the original feature vector, and the new feature vector is generally much smaller. Because of this reduction in the size of the feature vector, PCA is often referred to as a dimensionality reduction method. The mathematics behind PCA involves extracting the eigenvectors and eigenvalues from the data covariance matrix. These methods are taught in linear algebra courses and will not be discussed here. You can think of PCA as rotating your data onto a new set of axes where the first dimension contains the greatest variance, the second dimension, the second highest variance, and so on. If we are looking at the relationships between six samples, each with a feature vector of 15,000 genes, PCA will produce a set of 6 transformed coordinate axes (Fig. 12.2). Not all of these new axes are equally important-the ones that represent (or explain) the largest amount of the original data variance best capture the relationships in the original data. PCA programs sometimes show only the first two principal components. Look carefully at the sum of the variance explained by the axes; if it is low (<70%) you should look at more than the first two principal components.
